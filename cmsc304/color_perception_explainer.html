Color perception: two classic illusions

Our visual system doesn't measure colors absolutely — it compares them to their surroundings. These two effects reveal how dramatically context shapes what we see.

Effect 1

Simultaneous contrast

Both center squares are the exact same color. Surrounding context shifts how we perceive them. Try both modes — the effect works with brightness and hue.

Contrast 85%

This effect is why a shadow on white paper looks different from gray paint, even when their RGB values are identical. The visual cortex is essentially doing local contrast enhancement — always comparing a region to its immediate neighbors.

The hue version works the same way: a neutral gray on a red background appears to pull toward red's complement (green), because the brain subtracts the dominant surround color to "normalize" the scene.

Effect 2

Bezold effect

Left half always shows white lines as reference. Pick a color for the right half. The amber squares are identical on both sides — only the thin grid lines differ.

Right half:

Right half: black lines. Same amber squares now appear darker and richer — almost burnt sienna.

Named after Wilhelm von Bezold (1873), who noticed that changing a single color in a textile pattern could make the entire rug appear to change. Unlike simultaneous contrast (which acts at the boundary of two regions), the Bezold effect works through optical mixing — the thin lines are close enough that the eye blends them with the surrounding area.

This is why the same orange pigment looks golden in sunlit rug photography and dark brown in shadow — the interleaving grout lines or weave threads shift the overall perceived tone.
